The Research That Guides This AI

This companion takes up an idea from this week’s workshop: that technology’s most promising role in writing is co-creation, where the person stays an active author rather than a reader of what the machine produces (Ensslin and Skains). It is adapted from The Undiagnosed: Story Space, a writing app from Dr. Megan Perram’s SSHRC-funded research, and reframed here for weekly student reflection. Open a section to see the studies and what they led to in this tool.

How The Companion Is Designed 5 Choices

Showing Who Wrote What

Draxler and colleagues ran two studies, with 30 and 96 people, on writing with AI. People often felt that sentences the AI wrote did not really belong to them, yet they still put only their own name on the finished piece. The more a person shaped the text themselves, the more it felt like theirs. In a reflective journal, being able to see where the words came from matters for students and for the instructors reading their work.

In This ToolThe AI’s replies appear in coloured bubbles inside the dark companion panel, and your own writing sits in the reflection box, so you can always tell who wrote what. Any words you accept from the AI show in purple in your reflection.

Draxler et al.

The AI Cannot Edit Your Writing

Huang and Saksono designed an AI storytelling assistant and decided on purpose that the AI would help people tell their story but would not write it, so the writing stayed authentic. Chan built a narrative chatbot around a “not-knowing” stance, where the person’s own account stays at the centre and the AI does not hand out answers.

In This ToolThe AI cannot type into your reflection. Its words only go in if you ask it to Suggest The Wording, press Add To My Reflection and then accept the change.

Huang and Saksono; Chan; Draxler et al.

Help With Getting Started

Jennings, a writing scholar with ADHD, finds AI most useful for the parts of writing that are hardest to begin: getting started, organizing and brainstorming. In an eight-week study with 20 students, Nepal and colleagues found that people preferred journaling prompts based on their own lives over generic ones. In a study of 30 people, Park and colleagues found that writing to a chatbot encouraged people to tell their experience as a story.

In This ToolHelp Me Get Started offers one small, specific place to begin, tied to one of this week’s questions and to your own experience, like a single slide or a moment you remember, rather than a big question about the whole topic.

Jennings; Nepal et al.; Park et al.

The AI Does Not Write Your Reflection

People do not feel that AI-written text is really theirs (Draxler et al.). Huang and Saksono, and Chan, both designed their tools so the AI helps but does not write. A reflection is meant to show a student’s own thinking, so a reflection written by the AI would miss the point of the assignment.

In This ToolThe companion will not answer the reflection questions for you. When you are looking for a word, it may offer two or three short options. You choose, and you type it yourself or ask it to suggest the wording for you to accept.

Draxler et al.; Huang and Saksono; Chan

Using Your Own Words

Rettberg shows that AI tends to flatten stories into the most common patterns it has seen. Draxler and colleagues found that the more people shaped a text in their own words, the more they felt it was theirs. Polished, generic phrasing can make every student’s reflection sound the same.

In This ToolThe companion mirrors your own language back to you instead of swapping in polished academic phrasing or its own interpretations.

Rettberg; Draxler et al.
