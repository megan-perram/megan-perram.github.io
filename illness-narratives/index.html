SSHRC Insight Development Grant · 2026–2028

Co-Authoring Illness Narratives with Generative AI

Insights from Women Experiencing Medically Unexplained Symptoms

The Undiagnosed Story Space App

This is a Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada (SSHRC) project funded through an Insight Development Grant (2026-2028). It explores the use of generative AI as an apparatus to facilitate illness narrative writing for women and gender diverse people living with medically unexplained symptoms (MUS), a term referring to distressing physical manifestations that cannot be identified and diagnosed pathologically. According to Statistics Canada, MUS is a highly prevalent condition, twice as likely to be reported by women, that sees multiple contacts with the healthcare system. The stigma associated with erroneous perceptions of frequent healthcare consultations has given rise to (gendered and raced) prejudicial beliefs around “frequent flyers” who either hystericize baseline bodily functions or seek prescriptions for pain medication.

Individuals living with MUS, who may not be receiving the answers they seek within the healthcare system and simultaneously encounter social stigma within the sociocultural take up of their illness without disease, may benefit from novel alternative pathways for responding to their illness. One such method that has an established record of efficacy within the literature is the practice of illness narrative, or autobiographical writing about the experience of illness or disability. Illness narrative scholars collectively speak to the transformative potential that writing about one’s experience of illness holds as a method to bring agency and voice back to the body in the wake of healthcare dehumanization (see Frank; Couser; Hawkins).

The primary objective of this project is to assess how a cohort of fifteen women and gender diverse people with MUS engage in co-authoring individual illness narratives with a generative AI application (developed with Anthropic’s Claude AI). This objective will be met through a specialized critical discourse analysis of the narratives and co-writing choices, alongside individual closing interviews exploring participants’ experiences, perceptions of accessibility, ethical considerations, and the program’s value as a supportive tool inside or outside the healthcare system. This work is fundamentally a humanities project rooted in the legacy of illness narratives as counter-practice and not formalized therapy, meaning it is a method for individuals to respond politically to their illness experience outside of the walls of the healthcare system. The analysis of the corpus will be done through a specialized critical discourse methodology that amalgamates broader disciplines including literary studies, digital humanities, critical code studies, and gender studies. Ultimately, it allows for the discursive examination of power dynamics and materiality of digital-born narratives.

The secondary objective of this work is to develop an initial, narrative practice-informed (White, Maps of Narrative Practice) framework for the ethical and effective use of generative AI as a co-author for the writing of MUS illness narratives. As there is no existing research in this specific area, this framework would be intended to inform future research and provide preliminary guidance to expand access for individuals seeking alternative ways to articulate their illness while serving as a conceptual scaffold to guide narrative therapy clinicians, community groups, and scholars exploring the possibilities of AI as a co-author in both narrative writing and clinical contexts.

This research will be an innovative and novel contribution to the fields of health humanities, digital humanities, and electronic literature that will stand as preliminary research to inform how marginalized communities can demand ethical relationships with AI and the healthcare systems they come into contact with.

Dr. Megan Perram · Campion College, University of Regina
